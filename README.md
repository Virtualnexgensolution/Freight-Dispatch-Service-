What a Freight Dispatch Service Actually Does
A professional dispatcher acts as an operational extension of your carrier business. Typical responsibilities include:

Searching load boards such as DAT and Truckstop
Researching lane rates and equipment requirements
Checking broker authority, insurance, and payment history
Negotiating line-haul rates and accessorial charges
Booking loads under your written authority
Scheduling driver check calls
Collecting bills of lading, rate confirmations, and proof of delivery
Supporting invoicing and factoring submissions
Documenting detention, layover, truck ordered not used, and lumper charges
Preparing weekly revenue and lane-performance reports

A dispatch service does not guarantee that every broker will pay. It also should not force you to accept a load, sign away control of your truck, or hide who pays whom.

The service’s legal position depends on what it does. A bona fide agent relationship requires a clear written agreement with the carrier. If a company solicits freight without a specific carrier, allocates loads among carriers, or otherwise performs broker functions, broker authority may be required. Review the FMCSA broker registration guidance and ask the provider to explain its operating model in writing.

How Freight Dispatch Service Pricing Really Works
A commonly quoted market range for percentage-based dispatch is 5% to 10% of gross revenue [VNS to confirm]. The percentage may apply to line-haul only, or it may include fuel surcharge and accessorial revenue. Never assume. Ask for the exact calculation.

A flat-fee model charges a set amount per week, per truck, or per load. A flat fee can be easier to forecast during a soft market because the dispatch cost does not rise with gross revenue. A percentage model may align incentives during a strong market because the service earns more when your booked revenue increases.

The right question is not, “Which fee is cheapest?” Ask, “What service level and revenue definition am I receiving for this fee?”

Clarify these points before signing:

Does the fee apply to line-haul, fuel surcharge, or both?
Does the service charge on detention and layover payments?
Are there onboarding, cancellation, or minimum-volume fees?
Does the carrier pay the dispatcher directly?
Does the broker pay the carrier directly?
Does the dispatch provider ever control freight funds?

Treat vague answers as a warning. A transparent provider should explain the fee in plain language and show an illustrative invoice before you commit.

The Vetting Protocol: How to Check a Broker Before You Roll
Broker verification is one of the most important reasons to use a freight dispatch service. A load is not profitable if the broker delays payment, changes the rate, or turns out to be part of a double-brokering scheme.

Ask your dispatcher to follow this sequence:

Confirm the broker’s legal identity. Match the company name, MC number, contact details, rate confirmation, and email domain.
Check FMCSA authority. Review registration status and confirm that the broker is authorized for the relevant property freight.
Review bond or trust information. FMCSA broker registration information identifies the $75,000 BMC-84 surety bond or BMC-85 trust requirement for property brokers.
Check insurance and operating history. Do not treat a new MC number as automatic proof of fraud, but apply extra scrutiny to young or recently reactivated authorities.
Review payment history. Ask whether the provider uses broker credit data, factoring eligibility, prior carrier experience, or an internal watchlist.
Confirm the payment terms. Record days-to-pay, paperwork requirements, quick-pay terms, and any deductions.
Identify double-brokering red flags. Watch for mismatched names, unusual email domains, pressure to use a different carrier identity, or refusal to provide a verifiable rate confirmation.

Ask the dispatch service what credit threshold or payment-history standard it uses. If the answer is “we take whatever is available,” you are not receiving meaningful risk control.

True Revenue Math: Rate Per Loaded Mile, Not Gross
Gross revenue can flatter a bad load. Calculate the economics across the entire trip.

Use this basic formula:

Net trip revenue = gross line-haul and approved accessorials − fuel − tolls − dispatch fee − other trip-specific costs

Then divide the result by total miles if you want a realistic trip-level yield. Track loaded-mile rate separately, but do not ignore deadhead, repositioning, waiting time, and unpaid miles.

Case Study: A dry van load pays $5,000 for 2,000 loaded miles. The truck travels another 300 miles to reach pickup. If the trip includes $1,100 in fuel, $180 in tolls, and an 8% dispatch fee of $400, the remaining amount before fixed costs is $3,320. Divided across 2,300 total miles, that equals approximately $1.44 per total mile.

Your actual fuel cost, tolls, insurance, maintenance, and operating expenses will differ.

Require your dispatcher to report:

Loaded miles
Deadhead miles
Total trip miles
Gross revenue
Revenue per loaded mile
Revenue per total mile
Fuel and toll assumptions
Accessorial revenue
Dispatch fees
Days-to-pay

If the weekly report shows only gross revenue, you are missing the numbers that matter.

Detention, Layover, and Accessorial Pay: Who Chases It?
Accessorial revenue often disappears because nobody owns the paperwork.

Require the driver or dispatcher to capture:

Arrival and departure times
Gate-in and gate-out records
Signed BOL notes
Appointment times
Receiver delays
Photos of waiting conditions when appropriate
Lumper receipts
Written broker approval for detention or layover

Define responsibility in advance. Decide who files the claim, who contacts the broker, who sends supporting documents, who follows up, and who confirms payment.

A freight dispatch service should maintain an accessorial log tied to each load. Do not accept “we will handle it” without a documented process.

Contract Terms That Protect You
Read the dispatch agreement before providing load-board credentials or signing a rate confirmation.

Require clear language covering:

Non-forced dispatch: You may reject any load without penalties.
Carrier control: You retain control of the truck, driver, equipment, and final load approval.
Fee structure: State the percentage or flat fee and exactly what revenue it applies to.
Payment flow: Brokers pay your carrier business directly unless a separate written arrangement applies.
Exit clause: Require a practical termination period, such as 7 to 30 days, with no unreasonable penalty.
Written scope: Define load sourcing, broker checks, check calls, paperwork, accessorial claims, and reporting.
Confidentiality: Protect your broker list, customer information, and operating data.

Refuse agreements that permit forced dispatch, conceal the fee calculation, demand unlimited exclusivity, charge unexplained penalties, or allow the provider to sign as the carrier without proper written authority.

Why Equipment Vertical Matters
A dispatcher who understands dry van freight may not understand reefer or flatbed operations.

For reefer, require knowledge of temperature instructions, produce seasonality, multi-stop appointments, washouts, rejected loads, and detention at refrigerated facilities.

For flatbed, require familiarity with securement requirements, tarping pay, permits, oversize restrictions, loading equipment, and route limitations.

For dry van, focus on drop-and-hook versus live loading, floor-loaded freight, appointment risk, trailer requirements, and receiver detention patterns.

Ask how many loads the service handles for your equipment type and which lanes it knows best. General freight experience is not the same as vertical specialization.
